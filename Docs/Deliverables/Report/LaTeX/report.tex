\fmheading{Abstract}

Most video games ship with gigabytes of files made by artists: 3D models, photographs for surfaces, recorded sounds. \textit{Sentinel} takes the opposite route. It is a complete game engine and an open-world military shooter in which \emph{nothing} is loaded from a model, image or sound file. Every tree, building, soldier, vehicle, surface, cloud and sound effect is created while the program starts, from short mathematical recipes and a random seed. Only the small text files holding the shader programs are read from disk.

The engine is written in the Odin language on OpenGL~4.1, the newest version macOS offers (it has no compute shaders), and is organised in six layers that may only depend on the layers beneath them. A deferred renderer records what every pixel is made of and then lights it with a sun and moon, spot and point lamps, cascaded shadow maps, ambient occlusion, screen-space reflections, an atmospheric sky with wind-driven clouds, fog, bloom, tone mapping, colour grading and anti-aliasing. Surfaces come from 18 texture recipes built on periodic gradient noise, domain warping and cellular noise; normal maps are derived from height fields with a Scharr filter. Objects and characters are tables of primitives, each placed by a chain of $4\times4$ transformation matrices (scale, rotate, move, hierarchy, view, projection), and lit by six selectable illumination models (Lambert, Phong, Blinn--Phong, Oren--Nayar, Cook--Torrance, subsurface) under directional, point, spot and area lights.

On top of the engine sit collision, a ladder state machine, four ground vehicles and a helicopter, enemy soldiers with stealth rules, synthesised audio, and a five-objective rescue mission in the spirit of \textit{Project I.G.I.} The work was measured and verified, not only drawn: at 1080p the GPU needs 10.9~ms per frame, and 281 unit tests, 30 GL checks, 8\,318 texture checks (including exact tiling of every material), 318\,567 renderer checks and two scripted full playthroughs pass. The report explains every technique in plain language, with its governing equations, diagrams and screenshots, and documents a debugging case in which a star in the fog colour painted a bright vertical line across the night scene.

\textbf{Keywords:} procedural generation, game engine, OpenGL, deferred rendering, physically based shading, noise, texture synthesis, real-time graphics.

\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Introduction}
Imagine a film studio that buys no props: a recipe book tells assistants how to build every tree and cloud, and a different ``seed'' gives a different but believable forest. \textit{Sentinel} is that studio for a game: a \emph{game engine} (the machinery that draws, simulates and sounds a world) plus a first-person military mission. \emph{Procedural} means shapes, colours and sounds are calculated by code, not stored in files. (A \textbf{GPU} is a second processor with thousands of tiny cores; a \textbf{shader} is the small program each core runs per pixel.)


\section{Background and Problem Statement}
Graphics courses teach lighting, textures and noise one at a time; a product needs them working \emph{together} 60 times a second, usually with art loaded from files. The problem: \emph{can a complete, good-looking, playable 3D game be produced by code alone on a student laptop?} Limits: no pre-saved model, image or precomputed file, and OpenGL~4.1 without compute shaders.

\section{Objectives}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=0.5in,label=\arabic*.,itemsep=0pt,topsep=0pt]
	\item Build a layered engine with downward-only dependencies.
	\item Generate all meshes, textures and sounds from code and seeds.
	\item Implement a modern renderer (PBR, shadows, sky).
	\item Build a playable game with vehicles, ladders, doors and a mission.
	\item Verify it with tests and a 16.7\,ms frame budget.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Works}
\textbf{Noise.} Perlin's noise~[1] blends random gradients on a grid; summing scales gives natural detail. Worley's cellular noise~[2] gives pebbles and leaves; domain warping~[13] bends patterns to hide the grid. Sentinel uses all three, made \emph{periodic} so textures tile.

\textbf{Shading.} Cook--Torrance with GGX~[3--5] is the standard reflection model; Oren--Nayar~[8] suits rough cloth.


\textbf{Pipeline and games.} Deferred shading~[14], cascaded shadows~[7], Hillaire's sky~[6], ACES~[9] and FXAA~[11] form the pipeline; \textit{.kkrieger} (2004) generated a shooter at run time and \textit{Project I.G.I.} (2000) inspired the mission.

\subsection{Transformations: Creating and Combining Objects}
Every object is a \emph{table} of parts. A part is a primitive (box, sphere, cylinder, cone, capsule, torus, wedge), optional deformers, a transform and a material. A point is written in \emph{homogeneous coordinates} $\mathbf p=(x,y,z,1)^{\!\top}$ so that moving, turning and scaling are all multiplications by $4\times4$ matrices and can be chained into one matrix:
\begin{equation}T(\mathbf t)=\begin{pmatrix}I&\mathbf t\\ \mathbf 0^{\top}&1\end{pmatrix},\quad S(\mathbf s)=\operatorname{diag}(s_x,s_y,s_z,1),\quad R_y(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&0&\sin\theta&0\\0&1&0&0\\-\sin\theta&0&\cos\theta&0\\0&0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.\end{equation}
$R_x$ and $R_z$ are the same pattern turned about their own axes. The transform of one part, as built by the engine, is
\begin{equation}M_{\text{part}}=T(\mathbf t)\;R_z(\gamma)\,R_y(\beta)\,R_x(\alpha)\;S(\mathbf s),\qquad \mathbf p_{\text{object}}=M_{\text{part}}\,\mathbf p_{\text{part}}.\end{equation}
Matrices act on the point from the right, so the order of events is \emph{right to left}: the part is first scaled (stretched), then turned about $x$, then $y$, then $z$, and finally moved. Reversing the order gives a different shape, because rotations and scaling do not commute.

\begin{plain}
Think of a LEGO model: each brick is made at the origin, squeezed or stretched, turned, and only then carried to its place. Because the \emph{last} step is the move, the turn happens around the brick's own centre, not around the world's centre.
\end{plain}

\begin{ex}A box vertex $(1,0,0)$ with $\mathbf s=(2,1,1)$, $\beta=90^{\circ}$ and $\mathbf t=(5,0,0)$: scaling gives $(2,0,0)$; $R_y(90^{\circ})$ maps $(x,z)\to(x\cos\beta+z\sin\beta,\,-x\sin\beta+z\cos\beta)$ giving $(0,0,-2)$; the move gives $(5,0,-2)$. (Checked by script.)\end{ex}

\textbf{Normals need a different matrix.} A surface normal must stay perpendicular to the surface. If the surface is stretched, applying $M$ to the normal would tilt it. The engine therefore transforms normals with the \emph{inverse transpose} of the upper $3\times3$ block $L$ of $M$:
\begin{equation}\hat n'=\frac{(L^{-1})^{\top}\hat n}{\lVert(L^{-1})^{\top}\hat n\rVert}.\end{equation}
For $S=\operatorname{diag}(2,1,1)$ and $\hat n=(1,1,0)/\sqrt2$: the correct normal is $(0.5,1,0)/1.118=(0.447,0.894,0)$, whereas naively applying $S$ gives $(0.894,0.447,0)$, which is no longer perpendicular to the stretched tangent $(2,-1,0)$ (dot product $1.34\neq0$; for the correct normal it is $0$). If $\det L<0$ the object is mirrored; the engine then reverses every triangle's winding and the tangent's handedness so faces still point outward.

\textbf{Combining parts.} All parts of an object that share a material are appended into \emph{one} mesh (each vertex multiplied by its part matrix), so a whole building costs one draw call per material, not one per brick. The finished object is placed in the world by one more matrix, $M_{\text{world}}=T(x,\text{ground},z)\,R_y(\text{yaw})$, and \emph{instancing} draws the same mesh at many placements, each with its own $M_{\text{world}}$ (one call for thousands of trees).

\textbf{Hierarchies.} Moving parts hang below their parent, so their matrices are \emph{products down the chain}. A tank's gun is
\begin{equation}M_{\text{gun}}=\underbrace{T(\mathbf p)R_y(\psi)R_x(-\phi)R_z(\rho)}_{\text{hull}}\;\underbrace{T(\mathbf c_t)R_y(\theta_t)}_{\text{turret}}\;\underbrace{T(\mathbf c_g)R_x(-\theta_g)}_{\text{gun}},\end{equation}
with hull position $\mathbf p$, yaw $\psi$, pitch $\phi$, roll $\rho$, turret pivot $\mathbf c_t$ and yaw $\theta_t$, and gun pivot $\mathbf c_g$ and elevation $\theta_g$. Turning the hull therefore carries the turret and gun with it, while the turret turns alone about its own pivot. A soldier's limb is built the same way from the posed joint positions: its matrix has $\mathbf t=$ the upper joint and its local $y$ axis along (lower joint $-$ upper joint), so each bone follows its joints.

\textbf{Deformers} are one-line mappings applied in the part's own space before $M_{\text{part}}$: \emph{taper} scales $x,z$ by a factor that varies linearly with height, \emph{twist} rotates by $\theta(y)=\Theta\,y/h$, \emph{bend} wraps the axis onto a circle of curvature $1/R$, and \emph{noise displacement} pushes each vertex along its normal by at most $A$,
\begin{equation}\mathbf p'=\mathbf p+A\,\nu(f\mathbf p)\,\hat n,\qquad \lVert\mathbf p'-\mathbf p\rVert\le A.\end{equation}
Soldiers' legs reach their targets by two-bone inverse kinematics: for bone lengths $a,b$ and hip-to-target distance $c$, the law of cosines gives the hip angle $\alpha$ between the target direction and the thigh,
\begin{equation}\cos\alpha=\frac{a^{2}+c^{2}-b^{2}}{2ac}.\end{equation}

\textbf{From the world to the screen.} Drawing a vertex multiplies it by the chain model, view and projection. The camera's \emph{view} matrix is a look-at transform (move the eye to the origin, turn the target onto $-z$), and the \emph{perspective} matrix with $f=1/\tan(\mathrm{fov}/2)$ divides by depth:
\begin{equation}\mathbf p_{\text{clip}}=P\,V\,M_{\text{world}}\,M_{\text{part}}\,\mathbf p,\qquad P=\begin{pmatrix}f/a&0&0&0\\0&f&0&0\\0&0&\tfrac{n+r}{n-r}&\tfrac{2nr}{n-r}\\0&0&-1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad \mathbf p_{\text{screen}}=\tfrac{(x,y)}{w}.\end{equation}
Here $a$ is the aspect ratio, $n,r$ the near and far planes, and $w$ the clip-space fourth coordinate (equal to the distance in front of the camera), so far objects shrink. Binocular zoom by a factor $k$ narrows the view: $\tan(\mathrm{fov}'/2)=\tan(\mathrm{fov}/2)/k$ (a $72^{\circ}$ view at $4\times$ becomes $20.6^{\circ}$).

\subsection{Procedural Textures}
Each material is a \emph{recipe}: a small shader that answers ``what is the colour, bumpiness, shininess and crevice-darkness at this point?''. Each recipe is baked at start-up into a 1024$\times$1024 picture (22 materials from 18 recipes: concrete, rock, sand, dirt, asphalt, metals, paints, wood, bark, leaves, needles, grass, canvas, camo, rubber, skin, glass) and keeps it on the GPU.


\emph{Gradient noise} (a rubber sheet pinned at random slopes) blends the four corner gradients of a grid cell with the smooth curve $s(t)=6t^5-15t^4+10t^3$, scaled by $\sqrt2$ so values lie in $[-1,1]$. Fractal noise sums octaves, each at double frequency and half strength:
\begin{equation}F(\mathbf x)=\frac{\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}2^{-i}\,\nu\!\left(2^{i}\mathbf x\right)}{\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}2^{-i}},\qquad F\in[-1,1].\end{equation}
Choosing gradients from the cell index \emph{modulo the period} $P$ gives $F(\mathbf x+P\mathbf e)=F(\mathbf x)$: the picture tiles with no seam (tested exactly). \emph{Domain warping} $F(\mathbf x+s\,[F_1(\mathbf x),F_2(\mathbf x)])$ swirls the pattern (stone, wood, soil); \emph{ridged} noise $\sum a_i(1-|\nu_i|)^2$ makes cracks and bark; \emph{cellular} noise gives leaves and pebbles (Fig.~\ref{fig:noise}).

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{images/noise_stages.png}
	\caption{Noise ingredients (illustration of the shader equations): gradient, fractal, warped, ridged, cellular.}\label{fig:noise}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Bumps without geometry.} A recipe's height map becomes a \emph{normal map} that fools the lighting into seeing bumps. The slope uses the Scharr $3\times3$ filter, the most direction-fair small filter, and crevices (height below the neighbours' average) darken the albedo and ambient occlusion:
\begin{equation}h_x\approx\frac{3(h_{NE}+h_{SE}-h_{NW}-h_{SW})+10(h_E-h_W)}{32\,\Delta\,N},\qquad \hat n=\frac{(-k\,h_x,\,-k\,h_y,\,1)}{\lVert\cdot\rVert},\end{equation}
where $\Delta$ is one texel and $N$ the cells per tile, so bump strength $k$ is scale-independent.
Triplanar mapping (weights $w_i=|n_i|^4\Big/\sum\limits_{j\in\{x,y,z\}}|n_j|^4$) avoids stretching.


\subsection{The Rendering Pipeline}
\emph{Deferred shading} photographs a ``data poster'' of the scene first (each pixel's colour, direction and shininess) and lights it once, which makes many lights cheap. The poster is the \emph{G-buffer}: albedo, octahedral-packed normal with roughness and metalness, emission with occlusion, and depth (from which position is rebuilt). Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} lists the passes. Distant things fade into haze, $C=T\,C_{\text{surf}}+(1-T)\,C_{\text{haze}}$, $T=e^{-kd}$. Finally the HDR image is compressed to screen range by the ACES curve~[9], graded, dithered~[10] and smoothed (FXAA~[11]):
\begin{equation}C_{\text{out}}=\operatorname{clamp}\!\Big(\frac{x(2.51x+0.03)}{x(2.43x+0.59)+0.14}\Big),\quad x=\text{exposure}\times\text{radiance}.\end{equation}

\subsection{Illumination Models and Light Sources}
The lighting pass visits every pixel of the data poster and adds up the contribution of each light, then the sky's ambient light and the surface's own emission:
\begin{equation}L_o=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{N_L}\;f(\mathbf l_j,\mathbf v)\;L_j\,\mathrm{att}_j\,\mathrm{sh}_j\,\max(0,\mathbf n\!\cdot\!\mathbf l_j)\;+\;\underbrace{\text{ambient}\cdot\mathrm{AO}}_{\text{sky}}\;+\;\mathbf e,\qquad N_L=\text{number of lights}.\end{equation}
Here $f$ is the \emph{illumination model} (the BRDF, the share of light from direction $\mathbf l$ that leaves toward the eye $\mathbf v$), $L_j$ the light's colour times intensity, $\mathrm{att}_j$ its distance falloff, $\mathrm{sh}_j\in[0,1]$ its shadow, $\mathbf n\!\cdot\!\mathbf l$ Lambert's cosine (light spreads over a larger area when it arrives at a slant), and $\mathbf e$ the emission of lamps and windows. Figure~\ref{fig:vectors} shows the four vectors every model uses; $\mathbf h=(\mathbf l+\mathbf v)/\lVert\mathbf l+\mathbf v\rVert$ is the half vector.

\textbf{The six illumination models} are chosen per pixel from the G-buffer (Fig.~\ref{fig:models}). With $\mathbf c_d$ the diffuse colour (metals have none) and $F_0$ the base reflectance (Subsurface replaces the lighting cosine, so it is written for that term):
\begin{align}
f_{\text{Lambert}}&=\frac{\mathbf c_d}{\pi}\\[4pt]
f_{\text{Phong}}&=\frac{\mathbf c_d\,(1-F_0)}{\pi}+F_0\,\frac{m+2}{2\pi}\,\left(\mathbf r\!\cdot\!\mathbf v\right)^{m},\qquad \mathbf r=2(\mathbf n\!\cdot\!\mathbf l)\,\mathbf n-\mathbf l\\[4pt]
f_{\text{Blinn--Phong}}&=\frac{\mathbf c_d\,(1-F_0)}{\pi}+F_0\,\frac{m+8}{8\pi}\,\left(\mathbf n\!\cdot\!\mathbf h\right)^{m}\\[4pt]
f_{\text{Oren--Nayar}}&=\frac{\mathbf c_d}{\pi}\Big(A+B\,\max\!\left(0,\cos\Delta\varphi\right)\sin\alpha\,\tan\beta\Big)\\[4pt]
f_{\text{Cook--Torrance}}&=\frac{(1-F_l)(1-F_v)\,\mathbf c_d}{\pi}+D\,V\,F\\[4pt]
\langle\mathbf n\!\cdot\!\mathbf l\rangle_{\text{Subsurface}}&=\operatorname{clamp}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf n\!\cdot\!\mathbf l+w}{1+w},\,0,\,1\right),\qquad w=0.5
\end{align}
The shininess exponent comes from roughness $\rho$: $m=\max\!\big(2/(\rho^{2}+10^{-4})-2,\,1\big)$ (roughness $0.5\Rightarrow m=6$). Phong's mirror direction $\mathbf r$ is cheap but needs a reflection per light; Blinn--Phong replaces it by $\mathbf h$, which is cheaper and behaves better at glancing angles, and the factors $(m+2)/2\pi$ and $(m+8)/8\pi$ normalise the highlight so it does not gain energy as it sharpens. \emph{Oren--Nayar} treats a rough surface as many tiny Lambertian facets that scatter light back toward the source, so cloth and concrete look flat and do not darken at the edge like Lambert: with $\sigma=\rho$, $A=1-\frac{0.5\sigma^2}{\sigma^2+0.33}$, $B=\frac{0.45\sigma^2}{\sigma^2+0.09}$, $\alpha=\max(\theta_i,\theta_r)$, $\beta=\min(\theta_i,\theta_r)$ and $\Delta\varphi$ the azimuth between light and view around the normal. \emph{Subsurface} lets light wrap a little past the shadow line (the cosine ramps from 1 down to 0 as $\mathbf n\!\cdot\!\mathbf l$ goes from 1 to $-w$), imitating light scattering inside skin. \emph{Cook--Torrance} is the physically based default: $D$ is the GGX facet distribution, $V$ the Smith visibility and $F$ Schlick's Fresnel,
\begin{equation}\begin{aligned}D&=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\pi\left((\mathbf n\!\cdot\!\mathbf h)^{2}(\alpha^{2}-1)+1\right)^{2}}, & F&=F_0+(1-F_0)\left(1-\mathbf v\!\cdot\!\mathbf h\right)^{5}, & \alpha&=\rho^{2},\\[4pt]
V&=\frac{0.5}{(\mathbf n\!\cdot\!\mathbf l)\sqrt{(\mathbf n\!\cdot\!\mathbf v)^2(1-\alpha^2)+\alpha^2}+(\mathbf n\!\cdot\!\mathbf v)\sqrt{(\mathbf n\!\cdot\!\mathbf l)^2(1-\alpha^2)+\alpha^2}}.\end{aligned}\end{equation}
In the game, cloth and canvas use Oren--Nayar, skin uses Subsurface, and everything else uses Cook--Torrance (Table~\ref{tab:models}).

\textbf{Light sources} (Fig.~\ref{fig:vectors}b) differ only in how $L_j\,\mathrm{att}_j$ is found. A \emph{directional} light (sun, moon) has no falloff and parallel rays. A \emph{point} light (lamp, muzzle flash, explosion) uses the inverse-square law multiplied by a smooth window so it reaches exactly zero at its range $R$ and can be drawn as a bounded sphere:
\begin{equation}\mathrm{att}(d)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\left(1-(d/R)^{4}\right)^{2}}{d^{2}+1}, & 0\le d\le R,\\[8pt] 0, & d>R.\end{cases}\end{equation}
A \emph{spot} light (floodlight, flashlight) multiplies this by a cone factor \begin{equation}c(\theta)=\begin{cases}1, & \theta\le\theta_i,\\[2pt]\left(\dfrac{\cos\theta-\cos\theta_o}{\cos\theta_i-\cos\theta_o}\right)^{2}, & \theta_i<\theta<\theta_o,\\[8pt]0, & \theta\ge\theta_o,\end{cases}\end{equation}
where $\theta$ is the angle from the spot axis, $\theta_i$ the inner and $\theta_o$ the outer cone angle. An \emph{area} light (ceiling panel) is a rectangle sampled as a $4\times4$ grid of point emitters, each with intensity$/16$ and weighted by its emission cosine, which gives soft highlights and soft shading. For $R=14$\,m: $\mathrm{att}(1)=0.50$, $\mathrm{att}(5)=0.037$, $\mathrm{att}(10)=0.0054$ and $\mathrm{att}(14)=0$ (checked by script, Fig.~\ref{fig:curves}).

\textbf{Shadows.} The sun's shadow uses three \emph{cascaded shadow maps}: the camera's view range is cut at
\begin{equation}z_i=\lambda\,n\Big(\frac{r}{n}\Big)^{i/N}+(1-\lambda)\Big(n+(r-n)\frac{i}{N}\Big),\quad i=1,\dots,N,\end{equation}
a blend of logarithmic cuts (sharp shadows nearby) and even cuts, with $\lambda=0.75$, $N=3$, $n=0.1$\,m, $r=100$\,m, giving $9.1$, $24.2$ and $100$\,m. A pixel is looked up in the cascade containing its depth, moved a little along its normal (removes self-shadow acne), and compared against the stored depth with eight rotated percentage-closer taps, so the edge is soft. Spot lights have their own shadow maps, short ``contact'' rays darken crevices, and \emph{screen-space ambient occlusion} (SSAO) darkens corners where the sky is hidden.

\textbf{Ambient light} is the sky seen from the surface. Diffuse ambient is the sky's cosine-weighted colour; specular ambient uses Karis's split-sum fit, $\text{prefiltered sky}\times(F_0\,A+B)$, where $(A,B)$ depends only on roughness and $\mathbf n\!\cdot\!\mathbf v$; rough surfaces see a blurred sky. Both are multiplied by the occlusion term AO and fade inside rooms (the room volumes mask the sky), so interiors are lit by their lamps and the light entering by doors.

\subsection{Sky, Time of Day and Clouds}
The sun's height follows astronomy, $\sin(\text{elev})=\sin\varphi\sin\delta+\cos\varphi\cos\delta\cos h$ (latitude $\varphi$, declination $\delta$, hour angle $h$). The sky colour comes from Rayleigh and Mie scattering~[6]; the moon and a star field take over at night. Clouds live on two planes, cumulus at $H=1500$\,m and cirrus at 7\,km. A view ray with direction $\mathbf d$ meets the plane at
\begin{equation}\mathbf P_{xz}=\frac{H}{d_y}\,(d_x,d_z)+\mathbf w\,t,\end{equation}
where $\mathbf w$ is the wind velocity: shifting the noise by $\mathbf wt$ makes the clouds drift (Fig.~\ref{fig:clouds}).

\subsection{Terrain, Trees, Physics and Gameplay}
\textbf{Terrain} is warped, ridged noise on a base plateau, streamed in 64\,m chunks. \textbf{Trees} (oak, pine, birch, cypress) are tables of limbs and leaf lumps; each instance gets its own size, lean and tint, and the vertex shader bends it as a cantilever, $\Delta_x=2s\,(0.04+0.1g)(y/9)^2$ ($\Delta_z$ is 35\% of it), where $g=\tfrac12+\tfrac12\sin(1.3t+0.05x+0.03z)$ is a travelling gust.

\textbf{Collision} uses rotatable boxes and a capsule player. Cars follow the bicycle model, $\dot\psi=v\tan\delta/L$, and engine power fades toward top speed, $\dot v=a_0(1-0.6|v|/v_{\max})$. \textbf{Ladders} are a four-state machine (none, mounting, climbing, topping out) in which the controller owns the body, so it cannot stick. A staged machine drives five mission goals; sounds are synthesised.



\textbf{Verification.} Unit tests check that a mesh is placed exactly by its matrix, that normals stay perpendicular under non-uniform scale, and that a mirror keeps winding outward. A GPU fixture shader that includes the \emph{real} GLSL checks every illumination model for reciprocity ($f(\mathbf l,\mathbf v)=f(\mathbf v,\mathbf l)$) and with a ``white furnace'' energy test, and checks light falloff, light shapes and sun shadows (acne, detached shadows).

\section{Conclusion}
Small data-driven techniques give a deterministic world.

\section{Intellectual Property Considerations}
All code, shapes, textures and sounds in Sentinel are original: because every asset is generated from formulas and seeds, there are no third-party models, images or recordings to license. The libraries used (Odin, GLFW, miniaudio) are open source under permissive licences, and the papers and notes listed in the references are cited wherever their ideas are used. The mission is inspired by, but not copied from, \textit{Project I.G.I.}: no names, maps or art are reused.

\section{Ethical Considerations}
The game is a work of fiction about an invented compound. Its figures are abstract, low-detail shapes, and it contains no real places, groups or people. It is intended for adult players. No personal data is collected, no network connection is made, and saved progress is a small local text file that the player can read or delete.

\section{Safety Considerations}
Muzzle flashes, explosions and the alarm can bother light-sensitive players, and loud sound can hurt hearing. For this reason brightness changes are smoothed by an eye-adaptation curve, flashes are short, and all sounds are mixed and limited before reaching the speakers. Players are advised to take regular breaks, and the pause menu is always available with one key. The game also never forces fast reaction: enemies give an audible warning before an alarm, and crouching makes the player quieter and harder to see.

\section{Legal Considerations}
Open-source licence terms are respected, and no proprietary code, trademark, logo or map is included. Violence is stylised, and the project makes no claim to real military accuracy. The report credits all cited work, and the course rules on originality are followed: the code was written for this course, and the earlier course version is kept as a clearly marked git tag.

\section{Impact on Societal, Health and Cultural Issues}
An open, readable engine lowers the barrier to learning graphics: a student can read one short file per feature, change a number and see its effect at once. This supports the course aim of understanding rendering by building it, and the table-driven content lets non-programmers adjust shapes and materials. Shooter games can encourage long play sessions, so the design favours stealth, crouching and rescue over constant shooting; the player is rewarded for avoiding detection and for freeing the hostage. No cultural or religious symbols are used, and the briefing text is neutral and fictional. The tactical map and compass help players who find navigation hard.

\section{Impact on the Environment and Sustainability}
Because no art files are downloaded, the whole project is a few megabytes of source code, which reduces storage and transfer energy; a conventional game of similar scope would ship hundreds of megabytes of models and textures. Display synchronisation caps the frame rate at the monitor's refresh rate and avoids needless GPU work, and the GPU finishes each frame in about two thirds of its time budget, so the laptop is not driven at full load.

\chapter{Conclusions}

\section{Summary}
Sentinel shows that a complete 3D game, from surfaces and shapes to weather and sound, can be generated entirely by code under an OpenGL~4.1 limit: a six-layer engine, a deferred physically based renderer, noise-based recipes, table-driven content, a mission game and a large test suite fit in about 21\,000 lines and run within a 60 fps budget (10.9\,ms of GPU time). Most of what looks like ``art'' is a few simple ideas applied consistently and tested.

\section{Limitations}
Models are blocky (primitives plus noise), and characters are simple. There is no true global illumination or ray tracing; indirect light and reflections are screen-space approximations. Foliage is lumpy volume, the beacon is a solid column, soldiers cannot pick up weapons, and tuning used scripts and screenshots.

\section{Recommendations and Future Works}
The engine can be extended with leaf cards with cut-out transparency for more realistic trees, screen-space global illumination, point-light shadows, anisotropic highlights for brushed metal, networked multiplayer and a larger user study of difficulty and comfort. A port to a modern graphics API with compute shaders would allow noise, terrain and even animation to be generated on the GPU, and would allow true ray-traced reflections.
